Every year the holidays move. Labor Day, Veterans Day, and the Thanksgiving break land on different weekdays, which
means a program that meets on Wednesdays loses a different number of sessions than one that meets on Tuesdays. Rebuild
a ten-week curriculum around that and you find yourself counting weekdays by hand, twice, for two different programs.

That was one afternoon of my job this fall. I am the Chief of Staff for Associated Students at San Diego State, where I
run two first-year leadership programs: FLEX, which runs all year, and FAST TRACK, which runs a semester at a time.
Between them there is recruitment, interviews, committee placement, a weekly session to prepare and deliver, and a
steady stream of written material.

It is exactly the kind of work people mean when they say AI will transform administrative jobs. So this semester I paid
attention to where it genuinely helped, where it created work, and where I decided it had no business being.

<Callout label="Key takeaways">
- The tasks AI handled best were the boring, checkable ones. Not the creative ones.
- The largest time sink, writing materials, turned out to be a drafting problem, not a thinking problem. That is where
  AI earned its place.
- Anything that decides an outcome for a specific student stays human, and there is research explaining why.
</Callout>

## The test I ended up using

After a few weeks I noticed I was asking myself the same four questions before handing anything over. Together they
work as a rough decision rule:

1. **Is this deterministic or is it judgment?** Counting Wednesdays between two dates has exactly one right answer.
   Deciding which session to cut does not.
2. **Can I check the output faster than I could do the work?** If verifying takes as long as doing it, there is no gain.
3. **Does a specific student's outcome depend on it?** Admission, placement, evaluation. That is a hard line.
4. **Does it touch student information?** Under FERPA, records a school maintains about a student are education
   records, and the rules that govern them do not change because the tool is new.[^ferpa]

The interesting thing is how often these disagree with intuition. The work that feels most tedious is not always the
work that should be automated, and the work that feels creative is sometimes the most mechanical.

| Task | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| Rebuilding the calendars around holidays | Good fit. Deterministic and instantly checkable |
| Drafting handouts, slides, and emails | Good fit. Draft from AI, final words from me |
| Mapping the curriculum to certificate requirements | Partial. Useful for coverage, not for what to cut |
| Reading and evaluating applications | Human. A student's opportunity depends on it |
| Running the hour in the room | Human. That is the actual job |

## Where it worked

### The calendar rebuild

This is the cleanest case. Two programs, ten sessions each, mapped against the university's published academic
calendar, with holidays knocking out different weeks depending on the meeting day. The rules are fixed and the output
is verifiable in a minute against a calendar anyone can look up.

That verifiability is the whole reason it works. The failure mode of a language model here is a confidently wrong date,
which is exactly the failure I wrote about last week in a very different setting.[^rag] A wrong date does not look
wrong. It looks like every other date. So every generated schedule got checked against the published calendar before it
went anywhere, and one holiday collision turned up that way.

Honestly, this task does not need a language model at all. It needs a small script that takes the academic calendar and
the program rules and produces both schedules, flagging any collision. That is the version I want to build before next
year, because the rules do not change even though the dates do.

### The written material

This is where I spend the most time, and it surprised me. Scripts, handouts, slide decks, emails to students,
recruitment posts. Every semester, largely the same material, rewritten.

What I learned is that most of that time was not thinking. It was formatting, restructuring, and re-explaining
something I had already decided. AI is genuinely good at that part. It takes a rough outline and returns something
with a shape, which is much easier to react to than a blank page.

The limit showed up quickly. Anything a student actually hears me say has to sound like me. A facilitator script is
performed out loud in a room of eighteen-year-olds, and generated text has a particular rhythm that falls flat when you
read it aloud: too balanced, too tidy, every paragraph landing on a neat conclusion. My rule now is that AI writes the
scaffold and I write the sentences students hear.

### Checking curriculum coverage

Our programs are supposed to line up with the university's leadership certificate, which has four core learning areas.
Asking whether a set of sessions covers four defined areas is a mapping question, and mapping is mechanical: here is a
list, here is another list, where do they connect?

What AI could not do was tell me what to cut. This year the curriculum moved away from panels toward activity-based
sessions. That decision depended on knowing which panels students actually remembered, which guests were hard to book,
and what the room feels like at 6pm on a Wednesday in November. None of that is in any document.

## Where I pulled it back

Applications are the line, and this is where my own practice was messier than the rule I just described.

I used AI on one part of the application: the written statements. With a stack of responses to the same few prompts,
it is genuinely hard to hold them all in your head at once. So I had a model read across the statements, group similar
answers together, and flag which responses were unusual. That part I would do again. Comparing many pieces of text
against each other is something software does better than a tired person at eleven at night, and it surfaced strong
answers I might have skimmed past in the middle of the pile.

I also asked it which candidates stood out, based on factors I gave it. That is the part I would do differently.

When University of Washington researchers tested language models on resume screening across three million combinations
of job, race, and gender, the models favored white-associated names in 85.1% of comparisons and female-associated names
in only 11.1%. For Black male-associated names against white male-associated names, the models preferred the
white-associated candidate in nearly every case.[^uw] Those systems were not built to be unfair. They were built to be
helpful, and they reproduced the patterns in the text they learned from. A model asked who stands out will answer with
confidence whether or not it has any business doing so.

What kept this from being a real problem is that the model never had the last word, or the first one. I read every
application myself, ran the interviews, and made every decision. Its output worked as a second pass over the
statements, not as a filter: nobody was cut because software did not like their writing.

Still, "who stands out" is one short step from "who should get in," and that step is the whole ballgame. Two changes
for next cycle. First, strip names and identifying details before any statement goes near a model, since names are
exactly what drove the bias in that study. Second, keep the questions descriptive instead of evaluative: ask what
themes come up across the statements, not who the strongest candidate is. The first question makes me a better reader
of the pile. The second asks a system with documented bias to make a judgment about someone's future.

There is a practical reason to keep it, too. Reading applications is how I learn the cohort before I ever meet them.
Skipping that would save an evening and cost me the thing the evening was for.

## What stays human, permanently

The hour in the room. The one-to-ones. The moment a student has to defend a choice out loud to the person sitting next
to them. Those are not tasks with outputs, and treating them as inefficiencies to be optimized misunderstands what the
programs are for.

That distinction matters more broadly than student government. The research on which jobs AI touches tends to find that
exposure concentrates in writing, information gathering, and routine analysis rather than whole occupations.[^gpts] In
practice that means the tedious edges of a job get faster while the core of it does not move much. My job did not
become less human this semester. It became less clerical, which is a different thing.

## Building the boring version

Two of these tasks deserve real tools rather than a chat window. Neither is complicated, which is the point.

**The schedule.** Everything I described about holidays and weekdays is arithmetic. The inputs are the campus holiday
list and the rules of each program. The output is two schedules and a warning if they collide. Written down, the whole
problem is about fifteen lines:

<p class="code-label">Generating both program calendars</p>

```python
from datetime import date, timedelta

# Straight from the published academic calendar, not from a model's memory
CLOSED = {date(2026, 9, 7), date(2026, 11, 11), date(2026, 11, 25)}

def sessions(start: date, weekday: int, count: int) -> list[date]:
    """The next `count` meeting dates on `weekday`, skipping campus closures."""
    dates, day = [], start
    while day.weekday() != weekday:      # 0 = Monday
        day += timedelta(days=1)
    while len(dates) < count:
        if day not in CLOSED:
            dates.append(day)
        day += timedelta(days=7)
    return dates

def collisions(a: list[date], b: list[date]) -> set[date]:
    """Days both programs would meet, which is what I want flagged."""
    return set(a) & set(b)
```

Run against this fall's dates, that reproduces our schedule exactly: ten sessions from late September to the second
week of December, with the two closed Wednesdays skipped. It also answers in one line a question I worked out by hand,
which is that putting the second program on Wednesdays collides every single week, while Monday clears every holiday.

That version has properties a chat window does not. It gives the same answer every time. It can be tested. When the
university publishes next year's calendar, one person updates one list and both schedules regenerate. And it cannot
hallucinate a Wednesday, which is the specific failure I was checking for by hand.

The general lesson: if a task has fixed rules and one correct answer, a language model is the least reliable way to
get it. Use the model to help write the script, then let the script do the work.

**The statement pass.** The version I would build for next cycle does the useful part without inviting the risky part.
Separate identity from text before anything is read by a model, and keep the mapping on my side:

<p class="code-label">De-identifying statements before analysis</p>

```python
import re

IDENTIFIERS = ("first_name", "last_name", "preferred_name", "email", "student_id")

def deidentify(application: dict) -> tuple[str, str]:
    """Return (anonymous id, statement text) with obvious identifiers removed."""
    text = application["statement"]
    for field in IDENTIFIERS:
        value = application.get(field)
        if value:
            text = re.sub(re.escape(value), "[redacted]", text, flags=re.IGNORECASE)
    return application["anon_id"], text
```

Then the analysis asks for structure rather than verdicts: group these statements by theme, list the themes, and show
which responses do not fit any of them. Unusual answers are what I want surfaced, and "unusual" is a property of the
text. "Strongest" is a judgment about a person.

Keeping the identity map outside the model is not just a privacy control, though it is that. It also changes what I
can ask. If the model does not know who wrote anything, the only questions available are questions about writing.

## The rule I would give another student leader

Before you hand something over, ask whether you could check the answer in under a minute, and whether anyone's outcome
depends on it being right. If it is checkable and nobody's future rides on it, hand it over. If it decides something
about a person, keep it. Most tasks sort themselves quickly once you ask in that order.
