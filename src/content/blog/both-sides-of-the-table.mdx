This month I read applications, built a rubric, and interviewed candidates for roles in Associated Students. I also
sat on the other side of it, applying for product management jobs and answering questions into a webcam with nobody
on the other end.

Doing both in the same few weeks is strange. The two chairs feel completely different, and the gap between them is
mostly information. As the person running the process, I know exactly what happens to an application. As an
applicant, I get silence and guesses.

So this post is about closing that gap: what the scanning layer actually does, tested rather than assumed, and what
running structured interviews taught me about the part that still comes down to people.

<Callout label="Key takeaways">
- The famous claim that most resumes are auto-rejected by software does not hold up. The real filter is quieter:
  systems rank, and humans stop reading.
- Layout matters more than keywords, but not the way the advice says. My two-column test parsed fine. A table-based
  layout scrambled the meaning.
- Wording matters a lot to a keyword ranker. Identical work scored 10.27 against a job posting when it used the
  posting's vocabulary, and 1.25 when it did not.
- Scored against 108 real resume and posting pairs, keyword ranking barely beat a coin flip at telling a human-judged
  good candidate from a bad one.
</Callout>

## Part 1: the scanning layer

An applicant tracking system is, underneath, three things: a database of applications, a parser that turns documents
into structured fields, and a search and ranking layer sitting on top. Recruiters query it the way you would query
any database.

The scary version of this, which you have read a hundred times, is that 75% of resumes are rejected by software
before a human sees them. That number has no research behind it. It traces back to a company that sold resume
optimization software in 2013, and no peer-reviewed study supports it.

The real finding is more interesting. In a Harvard Business School and Accenture study of hiring practices, 88% of
employers said their own systems screen out qualified, high-skilled candidates, and the researchers estimated 27
million workers in the United States are effectively hidden by these processes.[^hidden] Note who is being blamed
there. It is not a rogue algorithm. It is the criteria employers configure, plus the ranked list a recruiter stops
reading at row twenty.

That distinction changes what you can do about it. You are not trying to trick a robot. You are trying to survive
parsing, then rank high enough that a human reaches you.

### Gate one: can it read your resume

Parsing runs before anything else. If the parser mangles your document, the ranking layer never sees your best work.

The standard advice is to avoid two-column layouts. I wanted to check, so I built the same resume three ways, printed
each to PDF, and extracted the text the way a parser would.

The two-column version came out clean. Every section landed in the right order, left column first, then right. That
surprised me, and it means the common advice is at least partly wrong.

Then I tried a layout built on a table, the way many Word and Canva templates are. Here is what came back:

<p class="code-label">Table-based resume, as the parser reads it</p>

```text
SKILLS
SQL
Python
Roadmapping
EXPERIENCE
Product Management Intern, Summer
2026
EDUCATION
B.S. Management Information Systems,
SDSU, 2027
Shipped an internal tool used by
engineers.
Ran weekly feedback sessions.
CERTIFICATIONS
Google Project Management
Chief of Staff, Student Government
Ran recruitment and interviews.
```

Read that as a machine would. The accomplishments are now separated from the job they belong to. "Shipped an internal
tool" appears under Education. The Chief of Staff bullets float below Certifications. Nothing is missing, but the
structure that gives the words meaning is gone.

So the rule is not "avoid columns." It is "avoid layouts that interleave rows," which is specifically what tables do.

You can test your own resume in about ten seconds. Open the PDF, select all, copy, and paste into a plain text editor.
What you see is roughly what the parser sees. If you want to be precise about it:

<p class="code-label">Reading your own resume the way a parser does</p>

```python
from pypdf import PdfReader

text = PdfReader("resume.pdf").pages[0].extract_text()
print(text)   # if this reads out of order, so does the parser
```

### Gate two: ranking

Once parsed, your application gets scored against the posting. Classic systems do this lexically, with a ranking
function like BM25 that rewards documents sharing rare terms with the query.

I wrote two versions of the same experience. One uses the language of the job posting. The other describes identical
work in plain language, the way most people naturally write.

<p class="code-label">A minimal keyword ranker, the kind an ATS search uses</p>

```python
import math, re
from collections import Counter

def tok(s):
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9+#/]+", s.lower())

def bm25(query, doc, corpus, k1=1.5, b=0.75):
    N, avgdl = len(corpus), sum(len(d) for d in corpus) / len(corpus)
    df = Counter(t for d in corpus for t in set(d))
    tf, score = Counter(doc), 0.0
    for t in set(tok(query)):
        if t not in tf:
            continue
        idf = math.log(1 + (N - df[t] + 0.5) / (df[t] + 0.5))
        score += idf * tf[t] * (k1 + 1) / (tf[t] + k1 * (1 - b + b * len(doc) / avgdl))
    return score
```

The results were not close:

| Same experience, written two ways | Score against the posting |
| --- | --- |
| Uses the posting's vocabulary ("roadmap", "A/B testing", "SQL", "requirements") | **10.27** |
| Plain language ("build plan", "live trials of two versions", "pulled numbers from the database") | **1.25** |

The second version is arguably better writing. It is certainly clearer to a human. Against a keyword ranker it is
nearly invisible, because the only words it shares with the posting are "and", "from", "intern", "product", "the",
and "to".

This is not an argument for keyword stuffing, which human readers notice immediately and which newer systems handle
poorly anyway. It is an argument for describing your real work in the employer's vocabulary. If they say A/B testing
and you ran experiments, call them A/B tests.

### Case study: does keyword matching agree with humans?

The two-resume demo shows the mechanism. It does not tell you how much this matters in practice, so I ran a small
study.

I used a public dataset of resumes paired with job descriptions, where each pair carries a human label: Good Fit,
Potential Fit, or No Fit.[^dataset] I sampled 108 pairs, 36 of each label, and scored every resume against the
posting it was paired with, using the same BM25 ranker above. Then I checked whether the ranking agreed with the
human judgment.

If keyword ranking were a good proxy for fit, Good Fit resumes should score clearly higher. Here is what came back:

| Human label | Pairs | Median score | Mean |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| No Fit | 36 | 47.9 | 55.5 |
| Potential Fit | 36 | 58.3 | 67.7 |
| Good Fit | 36 | 53.9 | 61.7 |

The ordering is wrong. Resumes humans called a **Potential Fit** scored higher than the ones they called a **Good
Fit**, and the gap between Good Fit and No Fit is small compared to the spread inside each group.

Two numbers make it concrete:

- Pick a random Good Fit resume and a random No Fit resume. The Good Fit one scores higher **57% of the time**. A coin
  flip is 50%.
- **44% of Good Fit resumes scored below the median No Fit resume.** Nearly half the qualified people sort below the
  middle of the unqualified pile.

Of the ten highest-scoring resumes in my sample, three were Good Fit, five were Potential Fit, and two were No Fit.

It is not a length effect, which was my first suspicion. Average resume length was nearly identical across the three
groups: 771, 749, and 772 words.

What it measures is vocabulary overlap, and vocabulary overlap is a weak signal for whether someone can do a job.
That is the quiet version of the hidden worker problem: nobody was auto-rejected, the list was simply sorted badly,
and a recruiter reading the top twenty never saw the rest.

**Limitations, because this is a blog post and not a paper.** It is 108 pairs from one dataset, scored with a basic
BM25 implementation. Real systems add filters, knockout questions, and structured fields rather than ranking on raw
text alone, and I cannot audit how the dataset's labels were produced. I report aggregate numbers only and quote no
resume text, since these are real people's documents.

### What is changing underneath

Two shifts are worth knowing about.

The first is technical. Lexical search is being replaced or supplemented by embedding-based matching, the same
technique I wrote about for documentation retrieval.[^rag] Semantic matching means the plain-language resume above
would fare better, because "live trials of two versions" sits near "A/B testing" in vector space. It also means the
ranking becomes harder to inspect: there is no keyword list to point at, just a similarity score.

The second is legal, and it happened this year. Illinois now prohibits employers from using AI that has a
discriminatory effect in employment decisions, and requires them to tell you when AI is being used.[^illinois] New
York City requires annual independent bias audits of automated employment decision tools.[^nyc] Colorado's broader
AI law reaches employment decisions in June.[^colorado] The direction is consistent: disclosure, auditing, and
liability that follows the effect rather than the intent.

If you are applying in Illinois, you can ask whether AI is being used on your application. That is a right now, not a
courtesy.

## Part 2: the side of the table I was on

Reading applications and running interviews changed what I think the technical layer is even for.

Here is what we did. Every candidate got the same questions in the same order. Answers were scored against a rubric
written before we started. More than one person interviewed and scored. We took notes during and made decisions
afterward, comparing candidates against the rubric rather than against whoever we had just seen.

None of that was novel. It turns out it is also the format with the strongest research behind it. In the most recent
large reanalysis of selection methods, structured interviews came out at the top of the list, with a validity
estimate around .42 compared to roughly .19 for unstructured conversations.[^sackett] Same hour, same candidate,
twice the predictive power, purely from structure.

Having now done it, I think the mechanism is mundane. Structure does not make you smarter about people. It makes
candidates comparable, and it makes you write down why. When I had to score an answer against a criterion, I could no
longer justify a vague good feeling, and I could not let a strong candidate earlier in the day quietly set the curve
for everyone after them.

The other thing I noticed is that structure protects candidates from me. My memory of interview four is worse than my
notes from interview four. Without notes, the person I remember is the person who was most like me.

## Part 3: what this changed about how I apply

I write applications differently now, and it is not because I learned tricks.

**I use the posting's words for work I actually did.** Not because a robot demands it, but because both the ranker
and the tired human reading row twenty are looking for a match, and making them translate is my problem, not theirs.

**I check that my resume parses.** Ten seconds, copy and paste. I had never done this before this month.

**I treat one-way video interviews as a structured interview, because that is what they are.** Same questions, same
order, no follow-ups, scored afterward. The format that feels most alien to a candidate is the format with the best
research behind it, minus the part where someone can ask you what you meant.

**I stopped reading silence as a verdict.** Systems rank. Humans stop reading. The gap between "rejected" and "never
reached" is invisible from the outside, and treating them the same will drive you insane.

The part that has not changed is the part that matters. Every process I have been on either side of this month came
down to a person deciding they could picture working with you. The software decides who gets considered. People still
decide who gets picked.
